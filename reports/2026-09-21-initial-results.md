# Initial Jev Game Bench Results

On September 21, 2026, we ran Jev 1.13.0, OpenJev 0.1, and Kev-4B against random and scripted-heuristic baselines in Minesweeper, Tetris, and Pong. Every policy received the same three seeds, 100 through 102, at medium difficulty. Minesweeper allowed 81 decisions, Tetris allowed 200 pieces, and Pong allowed 500 decisions in lockstep mode, which excludes inference latency from the game physics. We recorded every decision and provider response. All 45 episodes completed with no invalid actions, provider errors, or timeouts. Three seeds are enough to test the benchmark and expose large differences, but not enough to establish stable model rankings.

Minesweeper produced a narrow and noisy separation because each board's first move automatically reveals a large empty region. The heuristic scored 69.67 safe squares on average and cleared one of three boards. OpenJev was the strongest learned model at 64.67, followed by Jev at 57.67 and Kev at 57.33; the random policy scored 58.00. None of the three learned models cleared a board. OpenJev's result is directionally promising, but its six-point lead over random comes from three episodes, while Jev and Kev were effectively tied with random. Future reports should retain clear rate beside revealed-square score and use a larger seed set before comparing close results.

Tetris gave the clearest ranking. The heuristic averaged 14,006.67 points and survived all 200 pieces in every episode. Jev led the learned models with 8,966.67 points and reached the piece limit twice, while OpenJev averaged 8,033.33 and reached it once. Kev averaged 66.67, topped out after 37 to 42 pieces in every run, and finished only modestly above the random policy's 26.67. On this task, Jev captured about 64% of the score gap between random and the heuristic, OpenJev captured about 57%, and Kev captured less than 1%. The result suggests that Jev and OpenJev can use the board features and placement choices coherently, while Kev-4B did not find a durable stacking policy under this prompt and action representation.

Pong reversed the learned-model order. The heuristic won all three matches and averaged the three-point maximum. Kev averaged 2.00 points, won one match, and scored 3, 2, and 1 across the seed pack. OpenJev averaged 1.00, matching random, and Jev averaged 0.67. Because these were lockstep matches against the same deterministic tracking opponent, network and inference delays did not change ball motion; the scores isolate action selection under the benchmark's discrete paddle controls. Kev therefore showed the best learned Pong policy in this sample even though its local inference was slower.

Taken together, the first run supports a game-by-game benchmark rather than one raw total: OpenJev led Minesweeper, Jev led Tetris, and Kev led Pong, while the heuristic remained the strongest policy in every game. Median decision latency ranged from 316 to 329 ms for Jev, 429 to 875 ms for OpenJev, and 927 to 3,220 ms for local Kev; Kev used slower reference kernels on the Apple M3 Pro, so those timings describe this deployment rather than the checkpoint alone. Jev consumed 1,036,530 input tokens, an estimated $0.0435 at the observed $0.042 per million input tokens; OpenJev used its free hosted allowance and Kev ran locally. The next publishable run should use at least 20 seeds, report confidence intervals, and add real-time Pong as a separate systems-sensitive condition while preserving these lockstep results.
